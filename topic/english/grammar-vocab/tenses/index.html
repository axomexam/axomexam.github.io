      English Tenses — Complete Rules, Chart and Exam Guide

      Tense is the form of a verb that shows when an action takes place — in the present, the past or the future. Tense questions appear in almost every competitive examination, either directly (fill in the blank, choose the correct tense) or indirectly through error spotting and sentence improvement. This guide explains all twelve tenses with their structures, signal words, rules and the mistakes examiners repeat year after year.

      What is a tense?

      English has three main times — present, past and future — and four aspects — simple, continuous, perfect and perfect continuous. Combining the three times with the four aspects gives the twelve tenses of English.

      Present tenses

      • Present Simple — habits, repeated actions, general facts, timetables. Signal words: always, usually, often, every day, generally, never.
      • Present Continuous — an action happening now or a temporary situation. Signal words: now, at present, currently, look!, listen!. Note: stative verbs such as know, believe, like, own are normally not used in the continuous form.
      • Present Perfect — a past action whose effect is felt now, or an action in an unfinished period of time. Signal words: already, yet, just, ever, never, since, for, so far.
      • Present Perfect Continuous — an action that started in the past and is still continuing. Signal words: since, for, how long.

      Past tenses

      • Past Simple — a completed action at a definite past time. Signal words: yesterday, ago, last week, in 1990.
      • Past Continuous — an action in progress at a past moment, or an action interrupted by another. Signal words: while, when, at that time.
      • Past Perfect — the earlier of two completed past actions. Signal words: before, after, by the time, already.
      • Past Perfect Continuous — duration of an action up to a point in the past. Signal words: for, since, how long.

      Future tenses

      • Future Simple — a prediction, promise or spontaneous decision using will; plans and intentions using going to.
      • Future Continuous — an action that will be in progress at a future time (at this time tomorrow, I will be travelling).
      • Future Perfect — an action that will be completed before a future time (by next June, he will have finished).
      • Future Perfect Continuous — duration of an action up to a future time (by next year, she will have been teaching for ten years).

      Signal words that decide the tense

      • since + point of time (since 2015, since Monday) → present perfect or present perfect continuous.
      • for + period of time (for two years) → perfect or perfect continuous.
      • ago, yesterday, last week → simple past.
      • already, yet, just, ever, never → present perfect.
      • while → past continuous for the longer action; when → simple past for the shorter action.
      • by the time → past perfect or future perfect.

      Sequence of tenses — important rules

      • If the main verb is in the past, the verb in the reported clause is usually also in the past (He said that he was ill).
      • Universal truths and habitual facts remain in the simple present even after a past reporting verb (He said that the earth revolves around the sun).
      • Do not use "will" after if, unless, when, until or before in a conditional or time clause (If it rains, we will stay — not "if it will rain").
      • For an action that began in the past and continues to the present, use the present perfect or present perfect continuous, not the simple past.

      Common mistakes in exams

      • Using the simple past with since or for (wrong: I am living here since 2015; correct: I have been living here since 2015).
      • Using will after if or when (wrong: When he will come, we will start; correct: When he comes, we will start).
      • Using the present continuous with stative verbs (wrong: I am knowing him; correct: I know him).
      • Using ago with the present perfect (wrong: I have met him two days ago; correct: I met him two days ago).
      • Confusing the past simple with the past perfect when two past actions are compared.

      How to prepare tenses for competitive exams

      • Learn the structures in the table first, then apply them to examples instead of memorising rules.
      • Practise error spotting and sentence improvement, which test tenses indirectly.
      • Keep a list of the signal words and match each one to its tense.
      • Attempt timed mock tests and revise in both English and Assamese.

      Frequently Asked Questions

      How many tenses are there in English?

      There are twelve tenses, formed by combining three times (present, past, future) with four aspects (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous).

      What is the difference between "since" and "for"?

      "Since" is used with a point of time (since 2015) and "for" with a period of time (for two years). Both are used with perfect tenses.

      Which tense is used with "yesterday"?

      The simple past is used with definite past time expressions such as yesterday, last week and ago.
