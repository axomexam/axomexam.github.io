    Idioms and Phrases

    An idiom is a group of words whose meaning cannot be worked out from the meanings of the individual words. If we add up the meanings of the words in the phrase to let the cat out of the bag, we do not reach the sense of the phrase, which is to reveal a secret. An idiom must therefore be learnt as a whole, as if it were a single long word. In the ADRE Grade III paper idioms are asked in two ways, by giving the idiom and asking for its meaning, and by giving a sentence with the idiom underlined and asking what it means in that sentence.

    Idioms are of several kinds, and knowing the kinds helps in learning them. Some are idioms of action, such as to break the ice, to let the cat out of the bag and to bury the hatchet. Some are idioms of feeling, such as to be over the moon and to be down in the dumps. Some are idioms of difficulty, such as to be in hot water and to be between the devil and the deep sea. And some are idioms of quantity or degree, such as a drop in the ocean and once in a blue moon. Grouping them in this way makes revision quicker.

    Many idioms come from everyday life and can be remembered by the picture they call up. To beat about the bush means to talk around a subject without coming to the point, a picture of a hunter beating the bushes instead of going to the animal. To blow one's own trumpet means to praise oneself, a picture of a herald announcing one's own arrival. To burn the candle at both ends means to use one's energy in two directions and so waste it, a picture of a candle lit at both ends and so burnt out quickly.

    Some idioms come from the sea and from old trades, and knowing the source fixes the meaning. To be all at sea means to be confused, from a ship out of sight of land. To sail close to the wind means to take a risk, from a ship sailing near the direction the wind comes from. To show one's true colours means to reveal one's real nature, from a ship raising its real flag. To be taken aback means to be surprised, from a sailing ship caught with the wind behind the sails. To know the ropes means to be experienced, from a sailor who knows the ship's ropes.

    Some idioms come from animals and birds, and these are among the most commonly asked. To let sleeping dogs lie means to avoid stirring up an old trouble. To bell the cat means to face a danger that others avoid. To kill two birds with one stone means to gain two results by one effort. To count one's chickens before they are hatched means to depend on a result before it is certain. To cry over spilt milk means to regret something that cannot be undone, and to be a wild goose chase means a hopeless search.

    Some idioms come from the body and from everyday objects. To keep one's fingers crossed means to hope for a good result. To turn a deaf ear means to refuse to listen. To pull one's socks up means to make a greater effort. To put all one's eggs in one basket means to risk everything on a single chance. To have a skeleton in the cupboard means to have a hidden and shameful secret. To burn one's fingers means to suffer loss through one's own action.

    Some idioms are built on the pattern of a verb with a fixed object, and a small change of the object makes the phrase meaningless. We say to make a beeline, not to make a line; to give the cold shoulder, not to give the cold hand; and to play second fiddle, not to play second violin. Learning the idiom with its exact object, and never substituting a synonym for the object, is essential, because the examiner often offers a wrong object as a distractor.

    Some idioms describe a person by a single quality in a vivid way. A dark horse is a person whose abilities are unknown. A wet blanket is a person who spoils the enjoyment of others. A blue-eyed boy is a person who is a favourite. A snake in the grass is a hidden enemy. A rolling stone is a person who changes his occupation often and so gathers nothing, and a bookworm is a person who reads all the time. These idioms are often asked in the form of a question that names a person and asks for the idiom.

    Some idioms have a meaning close to another idiom, and the examination tests whether the candidate knows the difference. To be in hot water means to be in trouble, while to be in the soup means the same thing; but to be in deep water means to be in serious difficulty, and to be in the dark means to be ignorant of something. To pull someone's leg means to tease, while to pull the wool over someone's eyes means to deceive. Knowing which of two similar idioms fits the sentence is the skill that is tested.

    A related group is the pair of idioms that look alike but differ in meaning. To be at a loose end means to have nothing to do, while to be at the end of one's tether means to have no patience or strength left. To break the ice means to begin a conversation, while to break the news means to tell something unpleasant gently. To make up one's mind means to decide, while to make up for something means to compensate. A list of such pairs is worth keeping.

    In the examination a few practical steps help. Read the sentence first, because an idiom usually carries its meaning in context, and the surrounding words often point to whether the sense is good or bad. If the sentence is about a sudden success, an idiom meaning a failure cannot fit. Test each option by putting it back into the sentence and reading the whole sentence aloud. And if the idiom is unfamiliar, look for a picture in it, since most idioms came from a concrete scene and the picture often suggests the sense.

    The common errors are worth naming. Candidates guess the meaning from the words instead of from the whole phrase, and so take the literal sense. They confuse an idiom with another idiom of similar sound. They choose an option that fits the words of the sentence but not the sense. And they do not notice that some idioms have both a literal and an idiomatic sense, so that to have cold feet means to be nervous and not to have cold feet in fact. Reading the sentence for the intended sense removes each of these errors.

    To revise, keep the idioms in groups, one group for each picture, sea, animals, body and everyday objects, and write each idiom with its meaning in one line. Add one example sentence for each idiom, because the example fixes the use. Revise the groups for ten minutes a day, and add every idiom met in a question paper. Because the same idioms recur year after year, a candidate who learns the groups well can answer the questions in seconds and treat this chapter as a place to gain marks quickly.

    The most productive way to learn an idiom is with its source or its picture. Grouping the idioms that come from the sea, from animals, from the body and from everyday objects makes them easier to remember. Writing one example sentence with each idiom also helps, because the example shows how the idiom is used in a sentence. The same idioms recur year after year, so this habit turns the chapter into a source of certain marks.

    One more point: some idioms carry both a literal and an idiomatic sense, and the context of the sentence decides which sense is wanted. Writing one example sentence for each idiom helps here too, because the example fixes the context in mind. Learnt this way, the idiom questions also become quick and certain.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Why can we not work out an idiom from its words?

    Because an idiom has a meaning of its own that is different from the meanings of the single words, as to let the cat out of the bag means to reveal a secret.

    What does to kill two birds with one stone mean?

    It means to gain two results by a single effort, as when one journey serves two purposes.

    What is a dark horse?

    A dark horse is a person whose abilities or intentions are unknown, often one who wins unexpectedly.

    How should idioms be learnt for the examination?

    In groups by their picture, such as sea idioms and animal idioms, with the meaning and one example sentence for each, revised daily.

    কিছুমান idiomৰ অৰ্থ আন এটা idiomৰ লগত ওচৰ, আৰু পৰীক্ষাই প্ৰাৰ্থীয়ে পাৰ্থক্যটো জানে নে নাই পৰীক্ষা কৰে। To be in hot water মানে কষ্টত পৰা, আৰু to be in the soup য়েও একেই বুজায়; কিন্তু to be in deep water মানে গুৰুতৰ কষ্টত পৰা, আৰু to be in the dark মানে কিবা নজনা। To pull someone's leg মানে জোকোৱা, আনহাতে to pull the wool over someone's eyes মানে প্ৰতাৰণা কৰা। দুটা একে ধৰণৰ idiomৰ কোনটো বাক্যত খাপ খায়, সেইটো জানাটোৱেই পৰীক্ষা কৰা দক্ষতা।

    সম্পৰ্কিত এটা গোট হৈছে একে দেখুৱা কিন্তু অৰ্থত বেলেগ idiomৰ যোৰ। To be at a loose end মানে কৰিবলৈ একো নথকা, আনহাতে to be at the end of one's tether মানে ধৈৰ্য বা শক্তি শেষ হোৱা। To break the ice মানে কথা আৰম্ভ কৰা, আনহাতে to break the news মানে অপ্ৰিয় কথা কোমলভাৱে কোৱা। To make up one's mind মানে সিদ্ধান্ত লোৱা, আনহাতে to make up for something মানে ক্ষতিপূৰণ দিয়া। এনে যোৰবোৰৰ এটা তালিকা ৰখাৰ যোগ্য।

    পৰীক্ষাত কেইটামান ব্যৱহৃত স্তৰে সহায় কৰে। প্ৰথমে বাক্যটো পঢ়ক, কাৰণ idiom এ প্ৰায়ে প্ৰসংগত তাৰ অৰ্থ বহন কৰে, আৰু ওচৰৰ শব্দবোৰে অৰ্থটো ভাল নে বেয়া সেইটো প্ৰায়ে দেখুৱায়। বাক্যটো হঠাতে সফলতাৰ বিষয়ে হ'লে বিফলতাৰ অৰ্থৰ idiom খাপ নাখায়। প্ৰতিটো বিকল্প পুনৰ বাক্যত ৰাখি গোটেই বাক্যটো জোৰেৰে পঢ়ি পৰীক্ষা কৰক। আৰু idiom অপৰিচিত হ'লে তাৰ ভিতৰত এটা ছবি বিচাৰক, কাৰণ বেছিভাগ idiom এটা মূৰ্ত দৃশ্যৰ পৰা আহিছে আৰু ছবিখনে প্ৰায়ে অৰ্থটো সূচায়।

    সাধাৰণ ভুলবোৰ নাম লোৱাৰ যোগ্য। প্ৰাৰ্থীয়ে গোটেই বাক্যাংশৰ পৰা নহয় শব্দবোৰৰ পৰা অৰ্থ অনুমান কৰি আক্ষৰিক অৰ্থ লয়। এটা idiomক একেই ধ্বনিৰ আন এটা idiomৰ লগত মিলাই পেলায়। এনে এটা বিকল্প বাছনি কৰে যি বাক্যৰ শব্দবোৰৰ লগত খাপ খায় কিন্তু অৰ্থৰ লগত নাখায়। আৰু কিছুমান idiomৰ আক্ষৰিক আৰু ৰূপক দুয়োটা অৰ্থ থাকে — to have cold feet মানে আতংকিত হোৱা, আক্ষৰিকভাৱে ঠাণ্ডা ভৰি থকা নহয় — এইটো লক্ষ্য নকৰে। বাক্যটোৰ উদ্দেশ্য অৰ্থৰ বাবে পঢ়িলে এই ভুলবোৰৰ প্ৰতিটো আঁতৰি যায়।

    পুনৰালোচনাৰ বাবে idiomবোৰ গোটত ৰাখক, প্ৰতিটো ছবিৰ বাবে এটা গোট — সাগৰ, জন্তু, শৰীৰ আৰু দৈনন্দিন বস্তু — আৰু প্ৰতিটো idiom তাৰ অৰ্থৰ সৈতে এটা শাৰীত লিখক। প্ৰতিটো idiomৰ এটা উদাহৰণ বাক্য যোগ কৰক, কাৰণ উদাহৰণে ব্যৱহাৰ নিশ্চিত কৰে। গোটবোৰ প্ৰতিদিনে দহ মিনিট পুনৰালোচনা কৰি প্ৰশ্নপত্ৰত লগ পোৱা প্ৰতিটো idiom যোগ কৰক। একেই idiomবোৰ বছৰে বছৰে পুনৰাবৃত্তি হয়, গতিকে যি প্ৰাৰ্থীয়ে গোটবোৰ ভালদৰে শিকে তেওঁ প্ৰশ্নবোৰ চেকেণ্ডত উত্তৰ দি এই অধ্যায়ক দ্ৰুত নম্বৰ পোৱাৰ ঠাই হিচাপে ব্যৱহাৰ কৰিব পাৰে।

    Idiom শিকোঁতে তাৰ উৎস বা ছবিটোৰ সৈতে শিকাটোৱেই আটাইতকৈ ফলপ্ৰসূ। সাগৰ, জন্তু, শৰীৰ আৰু দৈনন্দিন বস্তুৰ পৰা অহা idiomবোৰ পৃথকে গোট কৰি শিকিলে মনত ৰখা সহজ হয়। প্ৰতিটো idiomৰ অৰ্থৰ সৈতে এটা উদাহৰণ বাক্যও লিখি ৰাখক, কাৰণ উদাহৰণে বাক্যত কেনেদৰে ব্যৱহাৰ কৰিব লাগে তাক দেখুৱায়। একেই idiom বছৰে বছৰে পুনৰাবৃত্তি হয়, গতিকে এই অভ্যাসে এই অধ্যায়ক নিশ্চিত নম্বৰৰ উৎস কৰি তোলে।

    আন এটা কথা — কিছুমান idiom আক্ষৰিক আৰু ৰূপক দুয়োটা অৰ্থ বহন কৰে; বাক্যৰ প্ৰসংগ চাই কোনটো অৰ্থ লাগে নিৰ্ণয় কৰিব লাগে। প্ৰতিটো idiomৰ এটা উদাহৰণ বাক্য লিখি ৰখাটোৱেই এই কামত সহায় কৰে, কাৰণ উদাহৰণে প্ৰসংগটো মনত ৰাখে। এইদৰে শিকিলে idiom ৰ প্ৰশ্নও দ্ৰুত আৰু নিশ্চিত হৈ পৰে।

    • Idiom গোট হিচাপে শিকা হয়, শব্দে শব্দে নহয়।
    • To let the cat out of the bag মানে গোপন কথা প্ৰকাশ কৰা।
    • To beat about the bush মানে মূল কথালৈ নাহি ঘূৰি কথা কোৱা।
    • To kill two birds with one stone মানে এটা প্ৰচেষ্টাত দুটা ফল পোৱা।
    • To put all one's eggs in one basket মানে এটা সুযোগত সকলো লগোৱা।
    • A dark horse এনে ব্যক্তি যাৰ যোগ্যতা অজ্ঞাত।
    • To pull someone's leg মানে জোকোৱা, আৰু to pull the wool over the eyes মানে প্ৰতাৰণা কৰা।
    • Idiom তাৰ সঠিক object ৰ সৈতে শিকক: make a beeline, give the cold shoulder।

    সততে সোধা প্ৰশ্ন

    শব্দৰ পৰা idiom এৰ অৰ্থ কিয় উলিয়াব নোৱাৰি?

    কাৰণ idiom এৰ নিজা অৰ্থ থাকে যি পৃথক শব্দবোৰৰ অৰ্থৰ পৰা বেলেগ, যেনে to let the cat out of the bag মানে গোপন কথা প্ৰকাশ কৰা।

    to kill two birds with one stone মানে কি?

    ই মানে এটা প্ৰচেষ্টাত দুটা ফল পোৱা, যেনে এটা ভ্ৰমণে দুটা উদ্দেশ্য পূৰণ কৰা।

    a dark horse কি?

    A dark horse এনে ব্যক্তি যাৰ যোগ্যতা বা উদ্দেশ্য অজ্ঞাত, প্ৰায়ে যি অচানকি জয় লাভ কৰে।

    পৰীক্ষাৰ বাবে idiom কেনেকৈ শিকিব?

    ছবি অনুসৰি গোটত, যেনে সাগৰৰ আৰু জন্তুৰ idiom, প্ৰতিটোৰ অৰ্থ আৰু এটা উদাহৰণ বাক্যৰ সৈতে, প্ৰতিদিনে পুনৰালোচনা কৰি।
