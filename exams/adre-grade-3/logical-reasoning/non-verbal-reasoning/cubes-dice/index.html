    Cubes & Dice

    A cubes and dice question asks about the number of cubes in a solid, the number of painted faces, or the numbers on the faces of a die that are opposite each other. The chapter has two parts, the cube and the die, and each part depends on a small set of rules. A candidate who knows the rules will answer quickly, and a candidate who tries to picture the solid in the head will often slip.

    For a cube, the candidate should know that it has six faces, twelve edges and eight corners. A cube that is painted on the outside and then cut into smaller cubes has its small cubes in three kinds: those with three painted faces at the corners, those with two painted faces on the edges, and those with one painted face in the middle of a face. The candidate should count each kind separately.

    For a cube cut into n by n by n small cubes, the corners give eight cubes with three painted faces, the edges give twelve times the number of edges less the corners with two painted faces, and the faces give six times the square of one less than n with one painted face. The candidate should learn these counts, because the examiner uses the same pattern again and again.

    For the inner cubes, which have no painted face, the count is the cube of n minus two, because the outer layer is one cube thick. A cube of three by three by three has one inner cube, a cube of four by four by four has eight, and a cube of five by five by five has twenty-seven. The candidate should learn this pattern, because it is a common question.

    For a die, the candidate should know that the numbers on the opposite faces add to seven, so that one is opposite six, two is opposite five and three is opposite four. The candidate should use this rule to find the number on the opposite face, and should not try to picture the die in the head. The rule of seven is the whole of the die questions.

    A die question may show two or three views of the same die and ask for the number on the opposite face. The candidate should compare the views and should find the number that is common to two views, which tells which faces are adjacent, and should then use the rule of seven to find the opposite. The candidate should compare the views carefully and should not guess.

    A useful method for two views is to note the numbers that are adjacent in each view and to find the number that is adjacent to the same two numbers in both. The number that is missing from a view, given the rule of seven, is the one on the opposite face. The candidate should write the faces as they are seen and should fill in the opposites.

    The candidate should be careful with a question that gives the number of small cubes and asks for the size of the big cube, because the answer is the cube root of the number. A big cube of twenty-seven small cubes is three by three by three, and one of sixty-four is four by four by four. The candidate should know the cubes of the counting numbers up to ten, because the questions use them.

    A question may also ask how many small cubes have at least one painted face, which is the total minus the inner cubes. A cube of four by four by four has sixty-four small cubes and eight inner cubes, so fifty-six have at least one painted face. The candidate should work out the total and the inner count, and should subtract.

    Finally, practise the chapter with a set of cube and die questions, and write the counts for a cube of three, four and five. Ten minutes of this practice makes the rules familiar, so that in the examination hall the count is made in a minute. The chapter is short and the rules are simple once they are understood, and the marks come reliably.

    It helps to write the counts for a cube of three, four and five before the examination, because the same counts are used again and again. A cube of three has eight corners with three painted faces, twelve edge cubes with two painted faces, six face cubes with one painted face and one inner cube. A cube of four has eight, twenty-four, twenty-four and eight. A cube of five has eight, thirty-six, fifty-four and twenty-seven. The candidate should know these counts.

    A useful question is how many small cubes have exactly one painted face, because the count is six times the square of one less than n. For n equal to three the count is six times four, which is twenty-four, and for n equal to four it is six times nine, which is fifty-four. The candidate should use the formula and should check the answer against the count for a small cube.

    The candidate should be careful with a question that paints the cube on some faces only, because the count changes with the number of painted faces. A cube painted on two opposite faces has only the cubes on those two faces painted, and the count is different. The candidate should read the question for how many faces are painted, and should count for that number.

    For a die, the candidate should know the rule of seven and should use it for every question about opposite faces. The candidate should also know that a standard die has the numbers one to six, and that the sum of all the faces is twenty-one. A question may ask for the sum of the numbers on the visible faces, and the candidate should use the rule of seven to find the hidden faces.

    A useful method for two views of a die is to write the numbers of the first view in a row and the numbers of the second view in the next row, and to find the number that is in both rows. The two numbers that are common are adjacent to the same faces, and the candidate should use the rule of seven to find the opposite of each. Writing the views in rows keeps the comparison clear.

    A question may give three views of a die, which is enough to find all the opposite pairs. The candidate should compare the views two at a time and should find the numbers that are common, and should then use the rule of seven. Three views give three pairs, and the candidate should check that the three pairs are consistent with the rule of seven.

    The candidate should be careful with a die that is not standard, because the question may give the opposite pairs directly. The candidate should read the question for the pairs and should not assume the rule of seven. A question that gives the pairs is testing the candidate's care in reading, and the candidate should use the pairs that are given.

    A useful check is to test the answer against the rule of seven and against the views that are given. If the answer gives a number that is already visible in the same view, the answer is wrong. The candidate should check that the number found for the opposite face is not among the numbers visible in the view.

    The candidate should also handle a question about the number of small cubes that have at least two painted faces, which is the corners plus the edges. For a cube of four the count is eight plus twenty-four, which is thirty-two. The candidate should add the two counts and should check against the total number of small cubes.

    • ঠিক এটা ৰঙ কৰা মুখ হ'ল ছয় গুণ n ৰ এটা কমৰ বৰ্গ।
    • এটা মানক পাশাত একৰ পৰা ছলৈ থাকে আৰু মুখবোৰ একৈশলৈ যোগ হয়।
    • দুটা দৃশ্যৰ বাবে, সেয়া শাৰীত লিখি সাধাৰণ সংখ্যাবোৰ বিচাৰক।
    • বিৰুদ্ধ সংখ্যাটো একেই দৃশ্যত দেখা নাযায় নিশ্চিত কৰক।

    সততে সোধা প্ৰশ্ন

    এটা পাশাৰ বিৰুদ্ধ মুখবোৰৰ নিয়মটো কি?

    বিৰুদ্ধ মুখবোৰৰ সংখ্যা সাতলৈ যোগ হয়: এক ষহৰ বিৰুদ্ধ, দুই পাঁচৰ আৰু তিনি চাৰিৰ।

    চাৰি গুণ চাৰি গুণ চাৰিৰ এটা ঘনকত কিমানটা ভিতৰৰ ঘনক আছে?

    আঠটা, কাৰণ ভিতৰৰ গণনাটো চাৰিৰ পৰা দুই বিয়োগ কৰাৰ ঘন, যি দুইৰ ঘন।

    চাৰি গুণ চাৰি গুণ চাৰিৰ এটা ঘনকৰ কিমানটা সৰু ঘনকত ঠিক এটা ৰঙ কৰা মুখ আছে?

    চব্বিশটা, কাৰণ সেয়া ছয় গুণ চাৰিৰ পৰা দুই বিয়োগ কৰাৰ বৰ্গ, যি ছয় গুণ চাৰি।

    এটা মানক পাশাৰ সকলো মুখৰ যোগফল কিমান?

    একৈশ, কাৰণ একৰ পৰা ছলৈ যোগ কৰিলে একৈশ হয়।
