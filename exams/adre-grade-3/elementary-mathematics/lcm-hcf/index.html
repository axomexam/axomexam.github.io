    LCM and HCF

    The chapter on the highest common factor and the lowest common multiple is small in appearance but wide in reach, because almost every question that talks about things meeting again, things being divided equally, or things being filled to the brim is really a question about the HCF or the LCM. In the ADRE Grade III paper these two ideas are tested through short, practical problems, and a candidate who can tell at a glance which of the two is wanted will finish the section quickly.

    The highest common factor of two or more numbers is the largest number that divides each of them exactly, leaving no remainder. It is also called the greatest common divisor. The lowest common multiple is the smallest number that each of the given numbers divides exactly. For two numbers the product of the HCF and the LCM is always equal to the product of the numbers themselves, a fact that lets a candidate find one of the two when the other is known.

    The HCF is found by writing each number as a product of prime factors and taking the common factors with the lowest powers. The LCM is found from the same factorisation by taking every factor, common or not, with the highest power. For example, to find the HCF and LCM of twenty-four and thirty-six, write twenty-four as two cubed times three and thirty-six as two squared times three squared; the HCF is two squared times three, which is twelve, and the LCM is two cubed times three squared, which is seventy-two. The division method, in which the numbers are divided by common primes until no common prime remains, is a faster route when the numbers are large.

    Two numbers are co-prime when their highest common factor is one. Co-prime numbers need not themselves be prime; eight and fifteen are co-prime even though neither is prime, because they share no common factor other than one. The HCF of two co-prime numbers is one and their LCM is the product of the two numbers. This fact appears often in questions that ask for the least number which is exactly divisible by two given numbers.

    Word problems decide which of the two ideas to use by the sense of the question. If things are to be divided into equal groups, or the largest tile that can cover a floor exactly, or the greatest number that divides several numbers leaving the same remainder, the HCF is wanted. If things meet again, or bells toll together, or runners on a circular track come together again, or a number must be exactly divisible by several numbers, the LCM is wanted. Reading the question once more before starting the calculation saves the candidate from solving the wrong problem correctly.

    Remainder problems have their own small rules. To find the largest number that divides a, b and c leaving the same remainder r in each case, subtract r from each number and take the HCF of the results. To find the largest number that divides a, b and c leaving the same remainder but with the remainder unknown, take the HCF of the differences between the numbers. To find the least number which, when divided by a, b and c, leaves the same remainder r in each case, find the LCM of a, b and c and add r.

    The least number which leaves remainders that are each short of the divisor by the same amount is found by taking the LCM and subtracting that common shortfall. For example, the least number which when divided by six, eight and twelve leaves a remainder of five in each case is the LCM of six, eight and twelve, which is twenty-four, plus five, giving twenty-nine. The same question with a shortfall of one is the LCM minus one. Candidates should practise both forms until the difference is automatic.

    The LCM of fractions is the LCM of the numerators divided by the HCF of the denominators, and the HCF of fractions is the HCF of the numerators divided by the LCM of the denominators. These two results appear in the paper more often than candidates expect, and they are easy marks for anyone who remembers them. A whole number is treated as a fraction with denominator one when it is mixed with proper fractions.

    Decimal questions are handled by first converting the decimals to fractions or by shifting the decimal point in every number by the same number of places until all the numbers are whole. The HCF or LCM is then found for the whole numbers and the decimal point is restored. For example, the HCF of 0.6 and 0.9 is found by taking the HCF of six and nine, which is three, and shifting back one place to get 0.3.

    A square or cube number that must be divisible by several numbers is found by taking the LCM and then raising each prime factor to the next multiple of two for a square, or of three for a cube. The smallest square number divisible by four, six and ten is found by taking the LCM, which is sixty, writing it as two squared times three times five, and then making every exponent even, which gives two squared times three squared times five squared, that is nine hundred. The method is short once the idea of even exponents is understood.

    Time, distance and calendar questions all lean on the LCM. Bells that toll at intervals of six, eight and twelve minutes toll together after the LCM of those numbers, which is twenty-four minutes. Two runners on a circular track meet again after a time equal to the LCM of the times they take for one round. The number of times the hands of a clock overlap in a day is a classic application that rewards a candidate who is comfortable with the LCM. Recognising the pattern turns a wordy question into a one-line calculation.

    The most common mistakes in this chapter are worth naming. Candidates use the HCF where the question wants the LCM and the other way round, they forget to add the remainder in a remainder problem, and they take the HCF of the given numbers instead of the HCF of their differences. A second reading of the question, and a quick check that the answer is plausible, for instance that an LCM is larger than the largest number given, removes most of these errors and makes the chapter one of the safest sources of marks in the paper.

    • The HCF is the largest number that divides each of the given numbers exactly.
    • The LCM is the smallest number that each of the given numbers divides exactly.
    • For two numbers, HCF times LCM equals the product of the numbers.
    • Two numbers are co-prime when their HCF is one.
    • Use the HCF for equal division and the LCM for things that meet again.
    • For fractions, the LCM is the LCM of the numerators over the HCF of the denominators.
    • The least number leaving a common shortfall is the LCM minus that shortfall.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How do I know whether a problem wants the HCF or the LCM?

    If the question talks about dividing into equal groups or the largest measure, it wants the HCF; if it talks about meeting again, tolling together or being exactly divisible, it wants the LCM.

    What is the quickest way to find the LCM of two numbers?

    Write both numbers as products of prime powers and take every factor with its highest power; or use the fact that LCM equals the product of the numbers divided by their HCF.

    How do I handle a remainder problem?

    For the largest divisor leaving remainder r, subtract r from each number and take the HCF; for the least dividend leaving remainder r, take the LCM and add r.

    প্ৰতিটো সংখ্যাক মৌলিক উৎপাদকৰ গুণফল হিচাপে লিখি আৰু সৰু ঘাত থকা সাধাৰণ উৎপাদকবোৰ লৈ গ.সা.গু. পোৱা যায়। একে উৎপাদকীকৰণৰ পৰা সাধাৰণ হওক বা নহওক, প্ৰতিটো উৎপাদক আটাইতকৈ বেছি ঘাতত লৈ ল.সা.গু. পোৱা যায়। উদাহৰণস্বৰূপে, চৌবিশ আৰু ছত্ৰিশৰ গ.সা.গু. আৰু ল.সা.গু. উলিয়াবলৈ চৌবিশক দুইৰ তৃতীয় ঘাত তিনিগুণ আৰু ছত্ৰিশক দুইৰ বৰ্গ তিনিভৰ বৰ্গ হিচাপে লিখক; গ.সা.গু. হৈছে দুইৰ বৰ্গ তিনিগুণ, অৰ্থাৎ বাৰ, আৰু ল.সা.গু. হৈছে দুইৰ তৃতীয় ঘাত তিনিভৰ বৰ্গ, অৰ্থাৎ সত্তৰ দুই। সংখ্যাবোৰ ডাঙৰ হ'লে সাধাৰণ মৌলিকেৰে ভাগ কৰি যোৱা ভাগ পদ্ধতিটো দ্ৰুত উপায়।

    দুটা সংখ্যাৰ গৰিষ্ঠ সাধাৰণ উৎপাদক এক হ'লে তেওঁলোক সহ-মৌলিক। সহ-মৌলিক সংখ্যা নিজেই মৌলিক হোৱাৰ প্ৰয়োজন নাই; আঠ আৰু পোন্ধৰ সহ-মৌলিক যদিও দুয়োটাই মৌলিক নহয়, কাৰণ তেওঁলোকে একৰ বাহিৰে কোনো সাধাৰণ উৎপাদক ভাগ নলয়। দুটা সহ-মৌলিক সংখ্যাৰ গ.সা.গু. এক আৰু ল.সা.গু. সংখ্যা দুটাৰ গুণফল। এই তথ্যটো সঘনাই সেই প্ৰশ্নবোৰত দেখা দিয়ে যিবোৰে দুটা প্ৰদত্ত সংখ্যাৰে সম্পূৰ্ণৰূপে বিভাজ্য আটাইতকৈ সৰু সংখ্যা সোধে।

    শব্দৰ সমস্যাই প্ৰশ্নৰ অৰ্থৰ জৰিয়তে দুটা ধাৰণাৰ কোনটো ব্যৱহাৰ কৰিব লাগে সেয়া নিৰ্ধাৰণ কৰে। যদি বস্তুবোৰ সমান গোটত ভগ কৰিব লাগে, বা মজিয়াখন সম্পূৰ্ণৰূপে ঢাকিব পৰা আটাইতকৈ ডাঙৰ টাইল বিচৰা হয়, বা কেইবাটাও সংখ্যাক একে ভাগশেষ এৰি ভাগ কৰা আটাইতকৈ ডাঙৰ সংখ্যা বিচৰা হয়, তেন্তে গ.সা.গু. বিচৰা হয়। যদি বস্তুবোৰ পুনৰ লগ পায়, বা ঘণ্টাবোৰ একেলগে বাজে, বা বৃত্তাকাৰ পথত দৌৰুৱাবোৰ পুনৰ একেলগে আহে, বা এটা সংখ্যা কেইবাটাও সংখ্যাৰে সম্পূৰ্ণৰূপে বিভাজ্য হ'ব লাগে, তেন্তে ল.সা.গু. বিচৰা হয়। গণনা আৰম্ভ কৰাৰ আগতে প্ৰশ্নটো আৰু এবাৰ পঢ়িলে প্ৰাৰ্থীক ভুল সমস্যা সঠিকভাৱে সমাধান কৰাৰ পৰা ৰক্ষা কৰে।

    প্ৰতিটো ভাগশেষ ভাজকতকৈ একে পৰিমাণে কম হোৱা আটাইতকৈ সৰু সংখ্যা ল.সা.গু. উলিয়াই সেই সাধাৰণ অভাৱটো বিয়োগ কৰি পোৱা যায়। উদাহৰণস্বৰূপে, ছয়, আঠ আৰু বাৰৰে ভাগ কৰিলে প্ৰতিটো ক্ষেত্ৰত পাঁচ ভাগশেষ এৰি যোৱা আটাইতকৈ সৰু সংখ্যা হৈছে ছয়, আঠ আৰু বাৰৰ ল.সা.গু., অৰ্থাৎ চৌবিশ, যোগ পাঁচ, যাৰ ফলত ঊনত্ৰিশ। এক অভাৱৰ একে প্ৰশ্ন হৈছে ল.সা.গু. বিয়োগ এক। প্ৰাৰ্থীয়ে দুয়োটা ৰূপ অনুশীলন কৰা উচিত যেতিয়ালৈকে পাৰ্থক্যটো স্বয়ংক্ৰিয় নহয়।

    ভগ্নাংশৰ ল.সা.গু. হৈছে লৱবোৰৰ ল.সা.গু. হৰবোৰৰ গ.সা.গু.ৰে ভাগ কৰা মান, আৰু ভগ্নাংশৰ গ.সা.গু. হৈছে লৱবোৰৰ গ.সা.গু. হৰবোৰৰ ল.সা.গু.ৰে ভাগ কৰা মান। এই দুটা ফলাফল প্ৰাৰ্থীয়ে আশা কৰাতকৈ সঘনাই পত্ৰত দেখা দিয়ে, আৰু ইয়াক মনত ৰখা যিকোনো লোকৰ বাবে ই সহজ নম্বৰ। এটা পূৰ্ণ সংখ্যাক সঠিক ভগ্নাংশৰ সৈতে মিলালে হৰ এক বুলি ধৰা হয়।
